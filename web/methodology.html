Methodology & data notes

This project compiles a single, browsable record of water- and slope-driven hazard events in Nepal — landslides, floods, flash floods, GLOFs, debris flows and avalanches — from 1971 to the present, and adds a clearly-separated experimental section for modelled context. It is a research and information aid, not an operational warning system.

1. Sources

  • Nepal DRR Portal / BIPAD — incident records from ~2011 onward, with point coordinates and casualty / loss fields. The bulk of recent data.
  • DesInventar Sentinel (UNDRR) — the national disaster inventory, 1971–2013, recorded at district and (mostly) village level. Provides the deep historical tail and the only pre-2011 coverage.
  • OCHA HDX — modern administrative boundaries (77 districts, 775 municipalities) and 2023 district population.
  • Curated major events — a small hand-maintained file (manual_events.csv) for headline disasters that the automated feeds miss or enter only after a delay. Currently one row: the 26 August 2026 Langtang / Rasuwa cascade.

Full licensing and attribution: DATA_LICENSE.md.

2. Unified record

Every source is normalised to one schema: date (with a precision flag), hazard type, district, municipality, coordinates, a location-precision flag, and the casualty / house-damage fields where reported. Original labels, source URLs, GLIDE numbers and reporting sources are kept for audit.

3. Hazard classification

Source labels are mapped to seven categories. Everything outside the six water/slope hazards (BIPAD also logs fire, lightning, epidemics, road accidents, …) is dropped. "Heavy rainfall" is treated as a driver, not a discrete impact event, and is excluded to avoid inflating flood counts.

4. Location & precision

BIPAD records carry real coordinates. DesInventar records do not, so they are placed at the centroid of the reported village (preferred) or district, taken from the shapefiles bundled with the DesInventar export, with a small deterministic jitter so many events sharing one centroid do not stack on a single pixel. Each event carries geo_precision:

  • exact — a reported point (~52% of events)
  • village_centroid — placed at the village centroid
  • district_centroid — only the district was known
  • approximate — curated event, best-estimate point

On the map, centroid points render as hollow rings and contribute half weight to the heat layer; "Precise locations only" hides them entirely. Treat any non-exact point as "somewhere in this area".

5. De-duplication

Two passes. First, exact double-entries (same hazard, same date, same place, same headline losses) are collapsed. Then a spatial pass merges records of the same hazard within 2 days and 5 km of each other, keeping the one with the richer casualty data and recording both source names. Ties are broken by lowest record id, so a rebuild is byte-for-byte reproducible. This is a heuristic: it will occasionally merge two genuinely distinct nearby events, or miss a duplicate that was logged far apart.

6. Severity index

A single composite score drives the heat-layer weight, the marker size and the "worst event" pick:

score = 5·deaths + 5·missing + 1·injured
      + 0.2·houses_destroyed + 0.05·houses_damaged
      + 0.002·people_affected

class breaks: minor 0 · small <5 · moderate <25 · major <100 · catastrophic ≥100.

The weights are a judgement call, not a validated model — they exist to make one number comparable across events, with deaths dominating. If you need a different emphasis, recompute from the raw fields, which are all retained.

7. Known biases & limits

  • Reporting ramp. Recorded events per year rise sharply after ~2011. This is mostly BIPAD's improving coverage, not a real change in hazard. Compare decades, and per-capita rates, with care.
  • Coverage seam. DesInventar ends in 2013; BIPAD starts ~2011. The overlap is de-duplicated, but the pre-2011 record is thinner and district-level.
  • Sub-district geography. Nepal has no open ward (adm4) boundary dataset, so municipality (adm3) is the finest level available. Ward numbers survive only inside BIPAD event titles.
  • Provisional figures. Casualty counts for very recent major events change for weeks. The Langtang row was entered at 489 dead / 1,500+ missing (Nepal Police, 28 Aug 2026).
  • Not verified. All figures are as recorded by the original source.

8. Impact corridors

Each significant event (any with recorded deaths, or a moderate-or-worse severity class) has an impact view showing a downstream corridor. There are two kinds, and the page always says which you are looking at:

  • Modelled reach (dashed) — the event point is snapped to the nearest reach of the HydroRIVERS network and routed downstream along that network's flow topology for a distance scaled by the event's severity score (3–120 km). The course is a real mapped river; the distance is an estimate.
  • Documented reach (solid, with a citation) — same routing, but the distance comes from published reporting rather than from severity, via data/raw/curated_reaches.json. For the 2026 Langtang cascade that is the reported ~100 km down the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli.

In neither case is the line hand-drawn: courses always follow the mapped river network, because sketching a river by hand invites exactly the sort of error the tool is meant to avoid. Equally, neither is an observed inundation footprint — no open dataset records the true extent of Nepal's historical events, which is why the modelled corridor exists at all. The corridor shows a route, never a width. Municipalities listed along a corridor are exposure context — the units the path crosses — not a claim that each was affected by that event. Elevation profiles are sampled from SRTM 30 m via OpenTopoData.

9. Experimental layers

The experimental section is walled off and carries its own disclaimer. It contains: (D) a descriptive seasonal climatology from this dataset — not a forecast; (A) zonal summaries of published global susceptibility / flood-hazard models; (C) the NASA LHASA daily rainfall-driven nowcast; and (B) a curated dangerous-glacial-lake inventory paired with the historical record of its downstream river corridor. None of these predict a specific event.

10. Getting the data

Everything the site loads is a static file under data/processed/ — it doubles as an API:

  • events.geojson — every event, one Point feature each
  • events_by_district/<slug>.json — one district's events
  • district_index.json, palika_index.json — rollups
  • calendar.json, outlook.json, glof.json, susceptibility.json, nowcast.json
  • meta.json — build date, counts, span, latest event

The map's download button exports whatever is currently filtered as CSV + GeoJSON. Per-district and per-event downloads are on those pages.
